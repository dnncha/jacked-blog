19:T18d5,<p>Fixed percentages and autoregulated training are tools for different kinds of uncertainty. A percentage-based plan is easy to write and repeat. Autoregulation uses performance, RPE, RIR, or bar speed to decide whether the planned load is appropriate today.</p>
<p>The useful question is not “which method wins?” It is whether the method gives you a repeatable decision rule for your goal, exercise, and training environment.</p>
<h2 id="the-main-methods">The main methods</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Percentage-based loading:</strong> uses a current or estimated 1RM to prescribe a load.</li>
<li><strong>RPE/RIR:</strong> adjusts the load or reps using perceived difficulty or estimated reps left.</li>
<li><strong>APRE:</strong> uses performance on a set to adjust the next load.</li>
<li><strong>Velocity-based training:</strong> uses measured movement speed as an additional signal.</li>
</ul>
<p>All four can be implemented well or badly. A rigid percentage can work when the training max is current and the environment is predictable. An autoregulated rule can fail when RIR ratings are inconsistent or the app treats a noisy signal as certainty.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-2025-network-meta-analysis-found">What the 2025 network meta-analysis found</h2>
<p>A 2025 systematic review and network meta-analysis compared APRE, RPE-based training, velocity-based training, and percentage-based training for maximal strength (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40791980/">Huang et al.</a>). APRE ranked highly in the analysis, but the interpretation needs care:</p>
<ul>
<li>rankings such as SUCRA are probabilities within the included network, not a guarantee of superiority in a new lifter;</li>
<li>the back-squat analysis did not show moderate or large differences between interventions;</li>
<li>studies varied in exercise, supervision, participants, duration, and implementation;</li>
<li>the result concerns maximal strength, not automatic hypertrophy, injury prevention, or a commercial app.</li>
</ul>
<p>An earlier systematic review also found that autoregulated methods can be useful, while emphasizing variation in the underlying studies (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33776802/">Helms et al.</a>). The evidence supports autoregulation as a reasonable option—not as a mandate.</p>
<h2 id="why-it-can-be-useful">Why it can be useful</h2>
<p>Performance is affected by sleep, stress, illness, recent training, food, and exercise order. A rule that allows a load to stay the same, rise, or fall when the observed effort changes can reduce the mismatch between the plan and the day.</p>
<p>That is a practical advantage. It is not proof that autoregulation prevents injury or that it can identify “recovery capacity” directly. A sensible rule should still include pain, technique, and a human override.</p>
<h2 id="a-simple-rir-rule">A simple RIR rule</h2>
<p>For a hypertrophy-focused exercise:</p>
<ol>
<li>choose a rep range and a target such as 1–3 RIR;</li>
<li>complete the set with controlled technique;</li>
<li>keep the load if the target effort is met;</li>
<li>add a small amount only when the target reps are repeatable at the planned RIR;</li>
<li>reduce load or stop when pain, illness, or technique breakdown changes the task.</li>
</ol>
<p>For strength work, use a more conservative effort target and longer rest. The numbers are starting points, not universal optima.</p>
<h2 id="what-velocity-adds">What velocity adds</h2>
<p>Velocity can provide an objective signal when the device and movement are consistent. It still measures movement speed, not pain, sleep, or tissue readiness. A fixed velocity-loss threshold should therefore be tested in context rather than copied as a guarantee.</p>
<h2 id="the-product-question">The product question</h2>
<p>An app can make autoregulation easier by recording the set, calculating the next suggestion, and retaining the trend. It should make the rule visible, allow corrections, and avoid promising that it will prevent overtraining or optimize every session.</p>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>Autoregulation is a defensible way to account for daily performance variation. Current evidence suggests it can improve maximal-strength outcomes in some settings, but network rankings are not a universal leaderboard. Pick the simplest method you can apply consistently, monitor the actual result, and keep judgment in the loop.</p>

<h2 id="applying-this-article">Applying this article</h2>
<p>Use one transparent adjustment rule for a four- to six-week block. Keep the exercise and target effort stable, record the recommendation and what you actually did, and review whether decisions became more consistent.</p>
<h2 id="limits-of-the-evidence">Limits of the evidence</h2>
<p>Autoregulation trials are usually supervised interventions, not blinded tests of commercial algorithms. Results vary by exercise, outcome, training status, and the quality of effort ratings.</p>
