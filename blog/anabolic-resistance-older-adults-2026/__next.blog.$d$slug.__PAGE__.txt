19:T189c,<p>Anabolic resistance describes a reduced acute muscle-protein-synthesis response to a given feeding or exercise stimulus in some older adults. It is a research observation, not a statement that muscle stops responding or that every person over 40 needs the same diet.</p>
<h2 id="what-changes-with-age">What changes with age?</h2>
<p>A 2024 systematic review and exploratory meta-analysis of 79 controlled trials found that resistance exercise increased muscle protein synthesis in healthy adults, but the pooled response was smaller in adults over 50 than in younger adults. The authors also found substantial heterogeneity linked to age, loading parameters, muscle group, and training experience (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38716482/">MPS review</a>).</p>
<p>Possible contributors include lower habitual activity, changes in blood flow and digestion, inflammation, illness, medication, and differences in the exercise stimulus. “Impaired mTOR signaling” is one mechanistic hypothesis, not a complete diagnosis or a reason to self-prescribe a supplement.</p>
<h2 id="protein-enough-total-intake-flexible-meal-sizes">Protein: enough total intake, flexible meal sizes</h2>
<p>A review of meal protein requirements describes a practical 20–30 g serving for many adults and notes that age, physical activity, and resistance exercise modify the response (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926513/">meal-protein review</a>). A planning range around 1.6 g/kg/day is useful for many resistance-trained adults, but the appropriate intake depends on body size, energy balance, food preference, kidney health, and clinical context. It is not a universal prescription or a minimum that switches growth on.</p>
<p>The idea that every older adult must hit 35–40 g or a fixed leucine threshold at every meal is too strong. A study comparing 30 g and 90 g of beef found a greater acute MPS response to the moderate serving in both young and older participants in that protocol; it did not establish a universal upper limit or apply to every food (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19699838/">feeding study</a>). A 2023 systematic review also found that no single blood-leucine variable reliably predicted the post-exercise MPS response across age groups (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37537134/">leucine review</a>).</p>
<p>Spread protein across meals that are realistic and include a complete or complementary amino-acid source. A larger serving can be practical when a person eats fewer meals, has a lower-protein food, or is managing appetite. Do not discard protein because it exceeds an internet “ceiling.”</p>
<h2 id="resistance-training-remains-central">Resistance training remains central</h2>
<p>Older adults can increase strength, muscle size, and function with progressive resistance training. In a 12-week randomized trial, adding 20 g of whey to two lower-protein meals did not further improve training adaptations when the comparison meals already contained protein (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35240263/">trial in older adults</a>). This does not make protein irrelevant; it shows that extra powder cannot replace a complete diet and a supervised training stimulus.</p>
<p>Training should be adjusted for joint symptoms, balance, medical conditions, and recovery—not for an assumption that a person’s age dictates one load or rep range. A clinician or qualified exercise professional can help when frailty, osteoporosis, neurological disease, or major illness is part of the question.</p>
<h2 id="practical-checklist">Practical checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li>Set a sustainable daily protein target and review it with a clinician if kidney disease or another medical condition is present.</li>
<li>Include protein in three or more meals if that fits appetite and schedule.</li>
<li>Use resistance training with gradual load, repetitions, or volume progression.</li>
<li>Track strength, function, body mass, and recovery rather than an acute MPS headline.</li>
<li>Avoid aggressive dieting when preserving muscle is the priority.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Anabolic resistance is a real but variable research finding. It does not mean older adults cannot grow muscle, nor does it justify a fixed 1.6–2.0 g/kg rule, 35–40 g meal requirement, or leucine supplement for everyone. Adequate food, repeatable resistance training, and appropriate clinical care remain the stronger foundation.</p>

<h2 id="limits-of-the-evidence">Limits of the evidence</h2>
<p>Acute tracer studies, longer training trials, and clinical populations answer different questions. Results vary with age, health, diet, exercise, and measurement, so no single protein amount predicts one person’s muscle gain.</p>
