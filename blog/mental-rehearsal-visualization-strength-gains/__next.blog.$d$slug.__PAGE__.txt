19:T16b3,<p>Mental imagery means rehearsing a movement without completing the physical movement. You imagine the lift, the body position, the effort, and the successful execution. It is a legitimate motor-learning tool, but fitness headlines often turn it into a claim that visualisation alone builds muscle.</p>
<p>The evidence is more modest and more useful: imagery may help rehearse skill, attention, confidence, and neural aspects of strength. It cannot provide the mechanical loading needed to produce the same hypertrophy stimulus as resistance training.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-research-supports">What the research supports</h2>
<p>Reviews of mental imagery have found benefits for some strength outcomes, particularly when imagery is combined with physical practice or used during short periods when movement is limited. The studies differ in participants, tasks, imagery instructions, and outcome measures, so a fixed “10–15% gain” is not a defensible promise. <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27803622/">See the systematic review.</a></p>
<p>A separate meta-analysis found that the mental-practice literature is small and varied, with only a limited subset suitable for dose-response analysis. That is useful evidence for a potential adjunct, not a validated hypertrophy protocol. <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26556071/">Read the analysis.</a></p>
<h2 id="what-imagery-may-change">What imagery may change</h2>
<h3 id="movement-rehearsal">Movement rehearsal</h3>
<p>Imagining a familiar lift can help you rehearse the sequence, bracing, bar path, and attention cue. This is most plausible when the task has a meaningful skill component.</p>
<h3 id="strength-expression">Strength expression</h3>
<p>Some strength tests improve after mental practice, especially in novices, athletes learning a task, or people temporarily unable to train. A better test may reflect motor coordination or willingness to express force, not new contractile tissue.</p>
<h3 id="confidence-and-focus">Confidence and focus</h3>
<p>Imagery can be a pre-lift routine that narrows attention and makes a plan concrete. If it calms you or improves consistency, that is a valid practical reason to use it even when the hypertrophy effect is unknown.</p>
<h2 id="what-imagery-does-not-show">What imagery does not show</h2>
<ul>
<li>a visualised contraction is not equivalent to a loaded set;</li>
<li>small EMG or brain-activity changes are not proof of muscle growth;</li>
<li>a strength improvement is not automatically hypertrophy;</li>
<li>imagery has not been shown to create a universal protection against atrophy during injury or immobilisation;</li>
<li>a successful visualisation cannot override pain, medical restrictions, or poor technique.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="a-sensible-way-to-try-it">A sensible way to try it</h2>
<p>Use imagery as a short supplement to training, not as a replacement:</p>
<ol>
<li>Choose one lift or movement you already understand.</li>
<li>Imagine a controlled repetition from a first-person perspective.</li>
<li>Include setup, breathing, bracing, range, and a realistic effort—not a fantasy personal record.</li>
<li>Keep the mental rehearsal brief enough that it improves focus rather than delaying the session.</li>
<li>Compare sessions with and without it using a repeatable warm-up, technique note, or effort rating.</li>
</ol>
<p>On an injury or rehabilitation break, only use imagery within the plan given by the treating professional. It may support engagement with the movement, but it should not be used to test a painful joint or to pretend that tissue capacity has returned.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Mental imagery is a low-cost skill and focus aid. It may help strength expression and movement learning, particularly alongside physical practice or during constrained training. It does not build muscle by itself, replace progressive resistance training, or justify a fixed percentage claim. Use it if it improves execution, then judge it by repeated performance and adherence.</p>

<h2 id="limits-of-the-evidence">Limits of the evidence</h2>
<p>Imagery studies use different tasks, instructions, durations, participants, and strength tests. Evidence for skill or force expression should not be presented as evidence of long-term muscle hypertrophy.</p>
