28:T1e62,<p>Muscle-building advice often turns a useful average into a rigid rule. The evidence is more flexible: several routes can work, the outcome depends on the person and programme, and the measurement used in a study matters.</p>
<h2 id="myth-1-light-weights-cannot-build-muscle">Myth 1: Light weights cannot build muscle</h2>
<p>Low- and high-load resistance training can produce similar hypertrophy when the sets are sufficiently hard and the programme is appropriate. A 2021 systematic review found similar muscle hypertrophy across higher- and lower-load training, while higher loads produced greater 1RM strength gains (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33874848/">Lopez et al.</a>).</p>
<p>That does not make every light set effective. Low loads can be uncomfortable, require more repetitions, and make it harder to maintain technique. Use the load that lets you train the target muscle, repeat the movement, and recover from the week.</p>
<h2 id="myth-2-you-must-finish-protein-within-30-minutes">Myth 2: You must finish protein within 30 minutes</h2>
<p>Resistance exercise increases muscle sensitivity to protein, but the size and duration of that window vary with the previous meal, training status, and total diet. A nutrition-timing review concluded that total daily intake and overall distribution matter more than a narrow post-workout deadline (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28150472/">Aragon and Schoenfeld</a>).</p>
<p>Eat a protein-containing meal when it fits your day. There is no need to rush a shake because 31 minutes have passed.</p>
<h2 id="myth-3-more-weekly-sets-always-mean-more-growth">Myth 3: More weekly sets always mean more growth</h2>
<p>More training volume can help up to a point, but the relationship is not an instruction to keep adding sets forever. A dose-response meta-analysis found a positive average relationship between weekly volume and hypertrophy, with important limits from fatigue, study duration, exercise selection, and how volume was counted (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/">Schoenfeld et al.</a>).</p>
<p>Start with a recoverable amount, track performance and symptoms, and add work only when the trend suggests it is useful. “10–20 sets” can be a planning range, not a universal biological requirement.</p>
<h2 id="myth-4-time-under-tension-is-the-main-driver">Myth 4: Time under tension is the main driver</h2>
<p>Slowing a repetition increases its duration, but that does not automatically increase hypertrophy. A systematic review found similar hypertrophic outcomes across repetition durations from roughly 0.5 to 8 seconds, with very slow repetitions less practical and less well studied (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25601394/">Schoenfeld et al.</a>). Control the load and range; do not turn every set into a slow-motion test.</p>
<h2 id="myth-5-every-set-must-reach-failure">Myth 5: Every set must reach failure</h2>
<p>Failure is one way to ensure high effort, not a mandatory endpoint. A 2023 meta-analysis found no clear hypertrophy advantage for momentary failure over non-failure training, while an exploratory 2024 meta-regression found that estimated proximity to failure had a stronger relationship with hypertrophy than strength but cautioned that RIR estimates were uncertain (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/">Refalo et al.</a>; <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/">Robinson et al.</a>).</p>
<p>Leave repetitions in reserve when doing so preserves technique, lowers unnecessary fatigue, or improves the rest of the week. Push closer on stable accessory work if you tolerate it.</p>
<h2 id="myth-6-compound-and-isolation-exercises-are-opposites">Myth 6: Compound and isolation exercises are opposites</h2>
<p>Both can build muscle. Compounds train several joints and muscles efficiently; isolation exercises can add targeted work with less whole-body coordination. Exercise choice should reflect the target, equipment, skill, comfort, and the amount of fatigue you can recover from—not a loyalty test.</p>
<h2 id="myth-7-lengthened-partials-are-automatically-superior">Myth 7: Lengthened partials are automatically superior</h2>
<p>Training at longer muscle lengths is an active research area. A 2025 within-participant study found similar upper-body adaptations between lengthened partial and full-range training over eight weeks (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39959841/">Wolf et al.</a>). Full range can be valuable, and a partial may be useful for a specific exercise or limitation; neither should be forced through pain.</p>
<h2 id="myth-8-soreness-proves-growth">Myth 8: Soreness proves growth</h2>
<p>Delayed-onset muscle soreness is a symptom of a novel or demanding stimulus. It is not a reliable measure of hypertrophy, and chasing soreness can make the next productive session worse. Use performance, repeatable volume, technique, and longer-term size measurements instead.</p>
<h2 id="a-better-decision-rule">A better decision rule</h2>
<p>When advice says “optimal,” ask: optimal for what outcome, in which population, over what time, compared with which alternative, and measured how? Then apply the smallest change that can answer the question in your own training.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Hypertrophy is more robust than internet rules suggest. Loads, tempos, exercise types, protein timing, and effort all matter, but none is a magic switch. Build a repeatable programme, progress it gradually, eat enough protein and energy, and interpret research averages as starting points rather than commandments.</p>
