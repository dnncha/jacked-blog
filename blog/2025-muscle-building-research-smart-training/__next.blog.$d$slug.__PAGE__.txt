19:T18c9,<p>Research rarely overturns a whole training system with one headline. More often, a new study narrows a question, changes the confidence in a claim, or shows that two workable approaches are closer than their marketing suggests. The recent resistance-training literature is useful for that reason: it supports flexible programming, not a new set of commandments.</p>
<h2 id="volume-more-can-help-with-diminishing-returns">Volume: more can help, with diminishing returns</h2>
<p>A 2026 meta-regression of 67 studies and 2,058 participants modelled weekly volume and frequency while distinguishing direct from indirect sets. The probability that volume was positively related to strength and hypertrophy was high, but the fitted relationships also showed diminishing returns. Frequency was compatible with a negligible hypertrophy effect in the model, while strength showed a positive relationship with increasing frequency (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/">dose-response meta-regression</a>).</p>
<p>This does not identify an optimal 8–12 or 10–20 set range for every lifter. Set counting depends on the exercise, muscle, effort, and whether an indirect contribution is counted. Start with work you can recover from, then add or redistribute it when performance and recovery support the change.</p>
<h2 id="load-and-failure-several-routes-can-work">Load and failure: several routes can work</h2>
<p>A systematic review and network meta-analysis of resistance-training prescriptions found that higher loads were more specific to strength, while a range of prescriptions produced hypertrophy when compared with no exercise (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37414459/">prescription meta-analysis</a>). A separate analysis of proximity to failure found that closer-to-failure training may have a modest relationship with hypertrophy, but the modelling was exploratory and did not establish a universal RIR target (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/">proximity-to-failure meta-regression</a>).</p>
<p>The practical conclusion is not “take every set to failure.” Use loads and effort that let you produce repeatable, technically sound work. Heavier loading can be useful for strength and skill; moderate or lighter loading can be useful for hypertrophy when fatigue and exercise safety are managed.</p>
<h2 id="periodization-more-useful-for-strength-than-a-hypertrophy-guarantee">Periodization: more useful for strength than a hypertrophy guarantee</h2>
<p>A 2022 systematic review found a strength advantage for periodized over non-periodized training when volume was equated, but no clear hypertrophy difference. Undulating periodization also showed a strength advantage over linear periodization without a clear muscle-size advantage (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/">periodization meta-analysis</a>). Periodization can still help organize fatigue, exercise variation, and goals, but it is not proof that a lifter must change intensity every few weeks.</p>
<h2 id="supplements-measure-the-outcome-not-the-mechanism">Supplements: measure the outcome, not the mechanism</h2>
<p>Protein supplementation can modestly augment resistance-training gains when total intake is not already sufficient, with diminishing returns as intake rises (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/">protein meta-analysis</a>). That finding does not turn a supplement into a substitute for training, calories, sleep, or adherence. Creatine, caffeine, and other products each need their own evidence review; a new headline cannot be generalized across the category.</p>
<h2 id="a-sensible-way-to-apply-research">A sensible way to apply research</h2>
<ol>
<li>Identify the outcome: muscle size, maximal strength, performance, recovery, or adherence.</li>
<li>Match the study population and protocol to your own situation.</li>
<li>Keep the comparison fair by changing one major variable at a time.</li>
<li>Track load, repetitions, effort, range of motion, symptoms, and body-composition measures over a useful block.</li>
<li>Treat a group average as a starting hypothesis, not a personal guarantee.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Recent research supports a flexible model: volume usually helps but eventually gives smaller returns, frequency can distribute work without being a hypertrophy switch, loads and proximity to failure can be varied, and periodization is especially useful for strength planning. The “smart” approach is the one that produces repeatable, recoverable progress—not the one attached to the newest headline.</p>

<h2 id="limits-of-the-evidence">Limits of the evidence</h2>
<p>Meta-analyses combine heterogeneous exercises, loads, participants, and outcome measures. The findings support ranges and decision rules, not a universal program or a guaranteed response.</p>
