19:T1845,<p>The phrase “muscle protein synthesis refractory period” is often used to describe the time after a protein meal or workout when muscle protein synthesis (MPS) is thought to be less responsive to another stimulus. It is a useful hypothesis for discussing meal spacing, but the popular version—a precise 3–4 hour timer after a fixed 20–25 g dose—is stronger than the human evidence allows.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-acute-studies-actually-measure">What the acute studies actually measure</h2>
<p>MPS is a short-term measure of protein turnover over a particular sampling window. It can rise after resistance exercise and protein, then return toward baseline even while muscle remodeling continues. An acute MPS response is not a direct measurement of how much muscle a person gains over a training block.</p>
<p>A controlled feeding study found that a moderate serving of beef stimulated MPS in young and older adults, with no additional acute response from a much larger serving in that specific protocol (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19699838/">feeding study</a>). That does not establish a universal upper limit: meal size, protein source, body size, age, exercise, and the measured tissue all change the question.</p>
<p>A review of meal requirements offers a practical range of about 20–30 g of protein for many adults, while noting that resistance exercise and age modify the response (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926513/">meal-protein review</a>). A 2023 systematic review found that leucine dose was associated with acute MPS in older adults, but no single plasma-leucine variable reliably predicted MPS across participants (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37537134/">leucine systematic review</a>). Those findings argue for a range and a complete protein source, not a stopwatch.</p>
<h2 id="what-longer-training-studies-suggest">What longer training studies suggest</h2>
<p>Protein distribution can be reasonable without being magical. In a 12-week trial of 26 young men doing supervised resistance training, an evenly distributed pattern with a larger breakfast protein serving produced a numerically greater increase in lean tissue than a skewed pattern, but the primary between-group comparison was not conventionally statistically significant (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32321161/">trial</a>). In a 16-week weight-loss and resistance-training trial, distributing the same 90 g daily protein evenly versus skewing it toward dinner did not differentially change body composition (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28903957/">trial</a>).</p>
<p>In older adults, adding 20 g of whey to two lower-protein meals did not further improve 12-week resistance-training gains when the comparison diet already supplied those meals with protein (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35240263/">trial</a>). These results are not contradictory: meal pattern can matter in some diets and populations, but total intake and the whole training program remain more important than hitting an exact interval.</p>
<h2 id="a-flexible-way-to-distribute-protein">A flexible way to distribute protein</h2>
<ul>
<li>Set a daily protein target appropriate to body size, training, energy intake, age, and health.</li>
<li>Include a meaningful protein serving in three or more meals if that fits your schedule.</li>
<li>Space meals by appetite and routine; three to five hours is a practical option, not a biological requirement.</li>
<li>Include protein near training when convenient, but do not panic if the next meal is later.</li>
<li>Use larger servings when a meal is doing more of the day’s work, and do not discard food because it exceeds a supposed “MPS ceiling.”</li>
</ul>
<p>Training the same muscle twice in one day is a separate programming question. MPS timing alone cannot prove that twice-daily training is superior or unsafe; the total volume, exercise selection, recovery, and performance response decide that.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Protein and resistance exercise create repeated opportunities for MPS, but the refractory period is not a universal timer. Distribute enough daily protein across meals you can sustain, keep the exercise and diet consistent, and judge success by long-term strength, lean tissue, recovery, and adherence—not by counting perfectly spaced “pulses.”</p>

<h2 id="limits-of-the-evidence">Limits of the evidence</h2>
<p>Acute tracer studies and longer training trials measure different outcomes. Protein-distribution studies also vary in daily intake, energy balance, age, training status, and meal composition, so no single schedule should be sold as optimal for everyone.</p>
