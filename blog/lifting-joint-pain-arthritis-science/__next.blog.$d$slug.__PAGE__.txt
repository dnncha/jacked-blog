19:T1bf6,<p>Joint pain is not a single condition, and “arthritis” is not a complete diagnosis. A sore knee after training, radiographic osteoarthritis, tendon pain, an acute injury, and an inflamed or infected joint need different decisions.</p>
<p>That is why the useful question is not “should everyone lift through joint pain?” It is: what has been assessed, which movements are tolerated, and how can loading be progressed without ignoring a worsening symptom pattern?</p>
<h2 id="what-the-evidence-supports">What the evidence supports</h2>
<p>Resistance training is commonly used to improve strength and function in people with knee osteoarthritis. It can be delivered with different loads, and a high percentage of one-repetition maximum is not automatically required for a useful programme.</p>
<p>The START randomized clinical trial followed 377 adults with knee osteoarthritis for 18 months. High-intensity training did not significantly reduce pain or knee-joint compressive force more than low-intensity training or the control condition. That does not mean strength training is useless; it means “heavier is always better for arthritis” was not supported by this trial.</p>
<p>A 2022 randomized trial and a meta-analysis of high-intensity training also found no consistent advantage of high intensity over lower-intensity or routine care for pain, function, or quality of life. The best load is therefore constrained by the person's capacity, goals, symptoms, equipment, and ability to repeat the work.</p>
<h2 id="pain-is-information-not-a-verdict">Pain is information, not a verdict</h2>
<p>Pain can reflect sensitivity, load tolerance, inflammation, technique, stress, or an injury. It is not a direct measurement of cartilage damage, and a pain-free session is not proof that a joint is healthy.</p>
<p>Use a simple response check instead of a universal pain score:</p>
<ul>
<li>Did the movement become more controlled as you warmed up, or progressively worse?</li>
<li>Was swelling, heat, instability, locking, or loss of function present?</li>
<li>Did symptoms return toward baseline by the next day, or are they accumulating?</li>
<li>Is strength or range of motion declining across sessions?</li>
</ul>
<p>An unfamiliar symptom, rapidly increasing pain, a hot or very swollen joint, significant trauma, locking, giving way, fever, numbness, or unexplained weakness warrants medical assessment. Do not use a blog post to diagnose arthritis or decide whether an acute injury is safe to load.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-modify-training-without-abandoning-the-goal">How to modify training without abandoning the goal</h2>
<p>Modification is usually more useful than treating one exercise as universally safe or unsafe. A qualified clinician or coach can help select options such as:</p>
<ul>
<li>a machine, supported, or partial-range variation;</li>
<li>a different grip, stance, tempo, or range of motion;</li>
<li>a lighter load with more repetitions, if that is better tolerated;</li>
<li>fewer sets or longer rest while symptoms settle; or</li>
<li>an alternative movement that trains the same broad function.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not assume that a squat, deep range, overhead press, or running pattern is harmful for every person. Nor should a modification be forced if it reliably worsens symptoms. Change one variable at a time so the response can be understood.</p>
<h2 id="a-practical-progression">A practical progression</h2>
<p>Start with a load and range you can perform with controlled technique and a stable symptom response. Keep a short record of the exercise, load, repetitions, effort, symptoms during the session, and symptoms later that day or the next morning.</p>
<p>Progress only one of these at a time:</p>
<ol>
<li>repetitions;</li>
<li>load;</li>
<li>range of motion;</li>
<li>sets; or</li>
<li>exercise complexity.</li>
</ol>
<p>If the response worsens, return to the last tolerable version and reassess. “Push through” and “rest until perfect” are both crude rules; the useful middle ground is graded exposure with appropriate assessment.</p>
<h2 id="what-not-to-promise">What not to promise</h2>
<p>Strength training may improve strength, function, confidence, and symptoms for many people, but it does not guarantee cartilage repair, reverse every degenerative change, or remove the need for diagnosis and treatment. Sleeves, straps, mobility drills, and supplements may affect comfort or convenience, but they do not make an unsuitable load safe.</p>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>Many people with osteoarthritis can benefit from progressive resistance training, and the evidence does not require one universal intensity. Choose a tolerable starting point, monitor the response, progress gradually, and get persistent, severe, or unusual symptoms assessed.</p>

<h2 id="limits-of-the-evidence">Limits of the evidence</h2>
<p>Arthritis studies involve selected diagnoses, ages, training histories, supervised protocols, and outcome measures. Group averages do not prescribe a load for one person's joint. This article is educational and does not replace a medical or physiotherapy assessment.</p>
