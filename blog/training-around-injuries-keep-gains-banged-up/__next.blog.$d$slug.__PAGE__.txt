19:T217c,<p>An injury does not always require complete rest, but “train around it” is not a diagnosis or a permission slip to push through pain. The safe option depends on what happened, which tissue is involved, how the symptom behaves, and whether a clinician has given restrictions.</p>
<p>This guide is for general training decisions while you arrange appropriate care. It is not a rehabilitation protocol.</p>
<h2 id="first-screen-for-a-reason-to-stop">First, screen for a reason to stop</h2>
<p>Stop the session and seek prompt medical assessment for a major traumatic event, deformity, rapidly increasing swelling, inability to bear weight, marked loss of strength or range, joint locking, giving way, numbness, progressive tingling, fever, chest symptoms, or pain that is severe at rest. Seek urgent care for symptoms that suggest a serious systemic problem, such as profound weakness or dark urine after extreme exercise.</p>
<p>If the problem is new, sharp, localised, or worsening with the movement, do not use a warm-up as a test of whether you can “push through.” Arrange an assessment, especially when the pain involves a joint, tendon, spine, or neurological symptom.</p>
<h2 id="complete-rest-is-not-the-only-option-but-neither-is-automatic-loading">Complete rest is not the only option—but neither is automatic loading</h2>
<p>Disuse can reduce strength and function, but the speed and size of change depend on the tissue, the person, the degree of unloading, and the time involved. A headline such as “muscle starts wasting after 48 hours” is not a useful prescription for an injured lifter.</p>
<p>Some injuries benefit from relative rest and gradual exposure. Others require protection, immobilisation, or surgery. Evidence from resistance-training studies in healthy adults cannot be transferred directly to an injured tendon, ligament, bone, or joint. The clinician managing the injury should set the boundaries.</p>
<h2 id="a-conservative-way-to-keep-the-rest-of-the-programme-moving">A conservative way to keep the rest of the programme moving</h2>
<p>When the painful area is not being loaded and the movement is symptom-free, maintain general training with a reduced complexity and a lower risk of accidental compensation:</p>
<ul>
<li>train unaffected regions with exercises you can control;</li>
<li>use stable machines or supported positions when they reduce bracing demands;</li>
<li>keep loads and effort below the point where the injured area becomes involved;</li>
<li>avoid “testing” the injury through a heavier set at the end of the session;</li>
<li>monitor the next-day response, not only how the exercise feels in the moment.</li>
</ul>
<p>There is no rule that a shoulder problem means every row is safe, or that a back problem makes every leg press appropriate. A movement that appears to spare the painful area may still load it through stabilisation, range, or fatigue. Start with a professional’s restrictions and your own symptom response.</p>
<h2 id="modify-variables-one-at-a-time">Modify variables one at a time</h2>
<p>For a movement that has been cleared for gradual training, change one variable:</p>
<ol>
<li>use a shorter comfortable range;</li>
<li>reduce external load;</li>
<li>choose a slower, controlled tempo;</li>
<li>reduce hard sets or frequency;</li>
<li>use a more stable variation;</li>
<li>increase the demand only when function and symptoms remain stable.</li>
</ol>
<p>“Slow eccentrics,” partial repetitions, blood-flow restriction, and unilateral work can be useful tools in specific programmes. None is automatically safer, and BFR in particular should be taught and prescribed appropriately when used around an injury.</p>
<h2 id="pain-monitoring-is-not-a-universal-permission-rule">Pain monitoring is not a universal permission rule</h2>
<p>Rehabilitation programmes sometimes use pain-monitoring models, but the acceptable symptom response differs by condition. A 2/10 rule, a 24-hour rule, or a fixed 60–70% load can be inappropriate for a fracture, acute tear, postoperative restriction, or neurological problem.</p>
<p>If a cleared exercise causes a progressively worse response, changes your movement, or reduces function the next day, reduce the demand and contact the treating professional. If the response is stable and function is improving, progress the smallest variable in the agreed plan.</p>
<h2 id="cross-education-and-the-uninjured-side">Cross-education and the uninjured side</h2>
<p>Training the uninjured limb can produce a modest strength effect in the opposite limb through neural cross-education. It may be considered in some immobilisation or rehabilitation settings, but it does not replace local tissue healing, and the movement must still be safe for the person’s injury and restrictions.</p>
<p>Do not train the healthy side solely because an internet article promises that it will preserve the injured side. Ask the clinician whether it is appropriate and how it fits with surgery, immobilisation, pain, and the actual goal.</p>
<h2 id="returning-to-the-original-lift">Returning to the original lift</h2>
<p>Return is better treated as a progression than a date. A useful sequence is:</p>
<ul>
<li>restore comfortable everyday movement;</li>
<li>practise the lift with a stable, easy variation;</li>
<li>rebuild range and load separately;</li>
<li>reintroduce volume only when the response is stable;</li>
<li>return to speed, heavy effort, or unstable environments last when they are relevant to the goal.</li>
</ul>
<p>The exact order depends on the injury. A training log should include exercise, load, range, symptoms during the session, and function later that day and the next morning.</p>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>You may be able to preserve training momentum during an injury, but the correct strategy is diagnosis- and symptom-dependent. Keep unaffected work only when it is genuinely symptom-free, modify cleared movements conservatively, and get professional guidance for new, persistent, or serious symptoms. Training around an injury is a coordination problem, not a toughness test.</p>

<h2 id="limits-of-the-evidence">Limits of the evidence</h2>
<p>“Injury” covers many different tissues and diagnoses. General resistance-training and cross-education studies do not establish a safe programme for a specific injury, and symptoms cannot be diagnosed from text alone.</p>
