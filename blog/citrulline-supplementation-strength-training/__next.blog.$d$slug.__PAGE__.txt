19:T1361,<p>Citrulline is a non-essential amino acid that can raise circulating arginine and influence nitric-oxide biology. It is commonly used for a “pump” before hypertrophy training.</p>
<p>The useful distinction is between a training sensation, an acute performance test, and a long-term adaptation. Citrulline may change the first two for some people. It has not been established as a direct muscle-building supplement.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-mechanism-means">What the mechanism means</h2>
<p>After ingestion, citrulline can be converted to arginine, a substrate for nitric-oxide synthesis. This may affect blood flow and vascular responses. It does not guarantee that more oxygen or nutrients reach a muscle in a way that increases hypertrophy.</p>
<p>A pump is not a measurement of muscle protein synthesis, and muscle protein synthesis after one session is not a measurement of new muscle over a training block.</p>
<h2 id="what-research-shows">What research shows</h2>
<p>Resistance-exercise studies and reviews have produced mixed results. Some report more repetitions, a lower perceived effort, or a small improvement in a fatigue-sensitive test. Others show no meaningful difference in strength or volume.</p>
<p>The evidence is particularly vulnerable to overinterpretation because many articles highlight one positive trial. A percentage such as “10–15% more reps” is not a general expectation, and even a real acute increase would not establish that citrulline creates more hypertrophy.</p>
<h2 id="practical-use">Practical use</h2>
<p>Research often examines citrulline or citrulline malate before exercise, but the ideal commercial dose and citrulline-to-malate ratio are not settled. A label listing a large amount of citrulline malate may not reveal how much pure citrulline it contains.</p>
<p>If you want to test it, use a transparent product and follow its label. Compare several similar sessions, track completed reps and perceived effort, and change one ingredient at a time. Gastrointestinal symptoms, headache, or dizziness are reasons to stop. Ask a clinician before use if you take blood-pressure or nitrate medication.</p>
<h2 id="where-it-sits-in-the-stack">Where it sits in the stack</h2>
<p>Protein, energy intake, creatine, and progressive training have a stronger claim on a lifter’s budget. Citrulline is optional: it may be useful when the goal is high-volume training and the person values a possible small acute effect, but it is not necessary for strength or hypertrophy.</p>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Citrulline is a plausible, mixed-evidence pre-workout supplement. It may improve selected high-volume performance outcomes, but a pump or a single extra-rep result is not proof of better muscle growth, and there is no reliable basis for promising a fixed rep increase.</p>
<p>For a more established supplement, read <a href="/blog/creatine-monohydrate-science-2026">Creatine Monohydrate</a>. For a broader review of citrulline malate, see <a href="/blog/citrulline-malate-pre-workout-science-2026">Citrulline Malate</a>.</p>

<h2 id="applying-this-article">Applying this article</h2>
<p>Predefine the performance outcome, compare repeated similar workouts, and evaluate the product by its effect on training—not by pump intensity alone.</p>
<h2 id="limits-of-the-evidence">Limits of the evidence</h2>
<p>Citrulline studies vary in dose, formulation, exercise, participant, and outcome. Acute performance signals do not establish a long-term hypertrophy effect.</p>
