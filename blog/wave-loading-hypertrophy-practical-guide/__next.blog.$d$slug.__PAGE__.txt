19:T1524,<p>Wave loading varies a training variable—often load, repetitions, or volume—across a repeating pattern. A lifter might use a heavier session, a moderate session, and a higher-repetition session rather than trying to make every workout identical.</p>
<p>That can be a useful way to organise competing goals. It is not a special hypertrophy mechanism, and the evidence does not justify presenting a particular percentage, wave length, or deload calendar as universal.</p>
<h2 id="what-periodization-can-do">What periodization can do</h2>
<p>Periodization can help a lifter distribute fatigue, practise different tasks, and make a progression decision easier. The value is practical and organisational: a plan can be easier to repeat when hard work and recovery are deliberately arranged.</p>
<p>A systematic review and meta-analysis comparing linear and daily undulating programmes found similar hypertrophy between the models when the programmes were compared directly. A later meta-analysis of volume-equated periodized programmes likewise supports treating periodization as a way to structure training, not as proof that one pattern automatically grows more muscle.</p>
<h2 id="what-a-wave-might-look-like">What a wave might look like</h2>
<p>The following is an example of organisation, not a prescription:</p>
<p>| Session | Emphasis | Example target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Strength practice | a heavier load with controlled repetitions |
| B | Hypertrophy work | a moderate load and repeatable hard sets |
| C | Volume or technique | a lighter load, more repetitions, or an alternative exercise |</p>
<p>Choose the actual load from recent performance and effort, not from a percentage that ignores the day. Keep enough rest to perform the intended work, and reduce the plan if technique or recovery deteriorates.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-test-it">How to test it</h2>
<ol>
<li>Pick one or two movements where variation solves a real problem.</li>
<li>Keep the exercise setup stable long enough to compare performance.</li>
<li>Record load, repetitions, effort, rest, symptoms, and the total weekly work.</li>
<li>Progress one variable when the trend supports it.</li>
<li>Reduce volume or load when fatigue, pain, or performance makes the current wave unrecoverable.</li>
</ol>
<p>Do not add a wave because a plateau automatically means the nervous system needs novelty. Check sleep, energy intake, technique, exercise selection, workload, and measurement noise first.</p>
<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Treating percentages as more accurate than the estimated one-repetition maximum behind them.</li>
<li>Increasing the top of every wave without accounting for fatigue.</li>
<li>Calling every lower-load session a “deload” even when the total work is still high.</li>
<li>Changing exercises and loading at the same time, so the result cannot be interpreted.</li>
<li>Assuming more variety means more hypertrophy.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line">The bottom line</h2>
<p>Wave loading is a reasonable programming format when it improves progression, fatigue management, or motivation. It is not a requirement for breaking a plateau and has not been shown to be universally better than linear, undulating, or simpler volume-equated training for muscle growth.</p>

<h2 id="limits-of-the-evidence">Limits of the evidence</h2>
<p>Periodization studies use different definitions of volume, intensity, exercise selection, and adherence, and many include untrained or recreational participants. Evidence about linear versus undulating training does not validate every wave-loading template.</p>
