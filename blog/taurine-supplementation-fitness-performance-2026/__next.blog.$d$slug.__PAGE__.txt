19:T1626,<p>Taurine is a sulfur-containing amino acid that occurs naturally in the body and in foods such as meat and seafood. It is also added to many energy drinks. Its presence in skeletal muscle and plausible roles in cell-volume regulation, calcium handling, and antioxidant systems have made it interesting to sports researchers.</p>
<p>Those mechanisms do not make taurine an anabolic supplement. The relevant human question is whether supplementation improves a meaningful performance or recovery outcome in a reproducible way.</p>
<h2 id="the-2025-acute-performance-review">The 2025 acute-performance review</h2>
<p>A 2025 meta-analysis included 23 randomized trials with 308 participants. A single dose of taurine was associated with a small-to-moderate average improvement across mixed exercise-performance tests. However, the authors rated the certainty of evidence low to very low because of heterogeneity, imprecision, risk of bias, and prediction intervals that often included no effect.</p>
<p>The review did not establish a universal dose. Studies commonly used doses in the 1–6 g range, and no significant dose-response relationship was detected within that range. A signal around taking taurine about an hour before exercise was not robust in sensitivity analyses. Some subgroups looked positive while anaerobic capacity and muscular-endurance findings remained inconsistent.</p>
<h2 id="recovery-and-muscle-growth-are-separate-questions">Recovery and muscle growth are separate questions</h2>
<p>Earlier reviews described mixed findings for soreness, creatine kinase, and recovery. A randomized trial in nine male triathletes using 3 g/day of taurine with chocolate milk for eight weeks found no improvement in the measured performance parameters or several damage and inflammatory markers. A change in creatine kinase is not itself proof of faster recovery, and neither performance nor recovery evidence establishes increased muscle hypertrophy.</p>
<p>There is no good basis for claiming that taurine “feeds” muscle protein synthesis, creates a cell-volumization effect that builds muscle, or acts like creatine. Taurine may be worth studying for selected exercise contexts, but it is not a substitute for training, protein, calories, sleep, or medical care.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-make-a-responsible-decision">How to make a responsible decision</h2>
<ul>
<li>Decide which outcome you want to change: endurance, repeated high-intensity work, strength, soreness, or something else.</li>
<li>Check whether the study used taurine alone or a multi-ingredient energy drink. Do not attribute a combined product's result to taurine automatically.</li>
<li>If you try it, change one variable at a time and record the dose, timing, exercise, sleep, and result. A single good session is not a trial result.</li>
<li>Do not assume that a commonly studied dose is safe or appropriate for a person with kidney, heart, blood-pressure, medication, or pregnancy-related concerns. Ask a clinician or pharmacist when those factors apply.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="bottom-line">Bottom line</h2>
<p>Taurine may produce a small, context-dependent acute performance benefit, but the certainty is low and the long-term muscle-building claim is unsupported. Treat it as an optional experiment with a clearly defined outcome, not as a proven training powerhouse.</p>

<h2 id="applying-this-article">Applying this article</h2>
<p>If you test taurine, keep the experiment separate from caffeine or a new pre-workout blend, and stop if it produces unwanted symptoms. A clinician should guide supplement decisions when you have a medical condition or take medication.</p>
<h2 id="limits-of-the-evidence">Limits of the evidence</h2>
<p>The taurine literature is small and heterogeneous, with mixed exercise tests, doses, timings, products, and participant populations. Acute performance findings do not establish long-term changes in muscle size, health, or recovery.</p>
