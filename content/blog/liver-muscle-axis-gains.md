# The Liver-Muscle Axis: Physiology Without the “Hidden Gains” Claim

The liver matters to exercise because it helps regulate glucose, lipids, amino-acid metabolism, bile acids, and many circulating proteins. Skeletal muscle also sends signals to other organs during exercise. This liver-muscle communication is scientifically interesting, but it is not evidence that a “liver support” supplement will unlock hidden hypertrophy.

## What the liver contributes

The liver helps maintain blood glucose between meals and during activity, processes nutrients, and participates in the production or clearance of circulating molecules. It also releases hepatokines and other signals that can interact with muscle and metabolic tissues. Reviews of exercise-related organ crosstalk describe these pathways as a network of potential mediators rather than a single liver-to-muscle switch. [Read the review.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34981891/)

The liver also contributes to the endocrine environment, including the growth-hormone/IGF-1 axis. That does not mean a transient growth-hormone pulse, a liver marker, or an IGF-1 value predicts how much muscle a healthy lifter will build. Endocrine physiology is not a supplement shopping list.

## Where liver disease and muscle health overlap

Metabolic liver disease can coexist with insulin resistance, altered inflammation, reduced muscle quality, and sarcopenia. That relationship matters clinically, but it is not the same as saying that a healthy person with an ordinary training plateau has “poor liver function.” A recent review of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease describes the two-way relationship while emphasising disease context and clinical management. [Read the review.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40408301/)

If you have jaundice, persistent abdominal swelling, unusual bleeding, severe itching, dark urine, unexplained weight loss, ongoing fatigue, or abnormal liver tests, seek medical evaluation. Do not try to infer liver health from a gym session, a pump, or a home supplement stack.

## Practical habits that support both organs

### Train consistently

Regular exercise can improve cardiometabolic health and influence signalling between muscle and other tissues. Choose a training dose you can recover from; the goal is not to chase a specific hepatokine or post-workout blood value.

### Eat a sustainable diet

Adequate energy, protein, fibre-rich foods, and a varied diet are more defensible foundations than detoxes or “liver cleanse” products. Adjust energy intake to your goal and use alcohol in a way that does not undermine sleep, training, or health.

### Be careful with supplements and medication

Some supplements can cause liver injury, and “natural” is not a safety guarantee. Do not add milk thistle, TUDCA, vitamin E, or other products as a general muscle-building protocol. Medication changes, liver-disease treatment, and abnormal laboratory results belong with a qualified clinician.

### Treat protein as nutrition, not liver stress theatre

Healthy people do not need to avoid ordinary protein because the liver “cannot process it.” Conversely, a high-protein diet is not a treatment for liver disease. Individual needs depend on health, energy intake, diet, and training; clinical liver or kidney conditions require personalised advice.

## What to ignore

- exact liver-glycogen numbers presented as a daily lifting requirement;
- claims that a fatty liver automatically causes a fixed muscle-protein-synthesis deficit;
- claims that increasing IGF-1 with a supplement guarantees hypertrophy;
- detox protocols built around unverified products;
- any article that treats a mechanistic hepatokine result as a human training prescription.

## Bottom line

The liver-muscle axis is real physiology, especially relevant in metabolic disease and exercise research. For a healthy lifter, the actionable plan is ordinary: train consistently, eat a sustainable varied diet, limit alcohol if it harms recovery, review medications and supplements responsibly, and investigate symptoms or abnormal tests clinically. A liver-health supplement is not a shortcut to more muscle.

## Limits of the evidence

Organ-crosstalk research often measures pathways, circulating markers, or disease associations rather than long-term hypertrophy in healthy resistance-trained adults. This article is educational and does not diagnose liver disease or recommend changing treatment.
