Citrulline is a non-essential amino acid that can raise circulating arginine and influence nitric-oxide biology. It is commonly used for a “pump” before hypertrophy training.

The useful distinction is between a training sensation, an acute performance test, and a long-term adaptation. Citrulline may change the first two for some people. It has not been established as a direct muscle-building supplement.

## What the mechanism means

After ingestion, citrulline can be converted to arginine, a substrate for nitric-oxide synthesis. This may affect blood flow and vascular responses. It does not guarantee that more oxygen or nutrients reach a muscle in a way that increases hypertrophy.

A pump is not a measurement of muscle protein synthesis, and muscle protein synthesis after one session is not a measurement of new muscle over a training block.

## What research shows

Resistance-exercise studies and reviews have produced mixed results. Some report more repetitions, a lower perceived effort, or a small improvement in a fatigue-sensitive test. Others show no meaningful difference in strength or volume.

The evidence is particularly vulnerable to overinterpretation because many articles highlight one positive trial. A percentage such as “10–15% more reps” is not a general expectation, and even a real acute increase would not establish that citrulline creates more hypertrophy.

## Practical use

Research often examines citrulline or citrulline malate before exercise, but the ideal commercial dose and citrulline-to-malate ratio are not settled. A label listing a large amount of citrulline malate may not reveal how much pure citrulline it contains.

If you want to test it, use a transparent product and follow its label. Compare several similar sessions, track completed reps and perceived effort, and change one ingredient at a time. Gastrointestinal symptoms, headache, or dizziness are reasons to stop. Ask a clinician before use if you take blood-pressure or nitrate medication.

## Where it sits in the stack

Protein, energy intake, creatine, and progressive training have a stronger claim on a lifter’s budget. Citrulline is optional: it may be useful when the goal is high-volume training and the person values a possible small acute effect, but it is not necessary for strength or hypertrophy.

## Bottom line

Citrulline is a plausible, mixed-evidence pre-workout supplement. It may improve selected high-volume performance outcomes, but a pump or a single extra-rep result is not proof of better muscle growth, and there is no reliable basis for promising a fixed rep increase.

## Applying this article

Predefine the performance outcome, compare repeated similar workouts, and evaluate the product by its effect on training—not by pump intensity alone.

## Limits of the evidence

Citrulline studies vary in dose, formulation, exercise, participant, and outcome. Acute performance signals do not establish a long-term hypertrophy effect.
