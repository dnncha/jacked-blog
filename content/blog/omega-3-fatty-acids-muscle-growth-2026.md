# Omega-3s and Muscle Growth: Strength Signals, Not a Hypertrophy Shortcut

Omega-3 fatty acids—especially EPA and DHA—are relevant to general nutrition and cardiovascular health. Whether fish-oil supplements build more muscle is a narrower question, and the evidence is much less settled than supplement marketing suggests.

## What the best direct review found

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis identified only four randomized studies of omega-3 supplementation combined with resistance training. It reported a significant improvement in muscle strength compared with placebo plus training, but no significant effect on muscle mass. The authors framed the result mainly as a possible countermeasure against muscular dysfunction in older adults, not as a proven hypertrophy supplement.

That is a useful distinction: a strength result may reflect neural, functional, pain, or task-specific changes and does not automatically mean more contractile muscle tissue. The review was also small, so the estimate should not be treated as a settled effect for experienced lifters.

## Why the mechanism is not the outcome

Omega-3s can alter cell membranes and inflammatory mediators, and laboratory work has proposed effects on muscle-protein signalling and amino-acid sensitivity. Those mechanisms are plausible reasons to study the nutrient. They do not establish that a supplement will increase a person's muscle size, reduce soreness, or improve recovery.

Older-adult studies and studies of people with health or nutritional problems may not generalize to a young, well-fed lifter. A short-term marker, a walking test, and a one-repetition maximum are different outcomes from long-term hypertrophy.

## Practical decisions

1. Build the diet around adequate energy, protein, fruit and vegetables, and a training plan you can repeat. Omega-3 capsules should not displace those basics.
2. Prefer dietary sources such as oily fish when they fit your diet. Food frequency and portion guidance depends on the person and local health guidance.
3. If considering a supplement, check the actual EPA and DHA amounts rather than the total “fish oil” weight. Do not treat 2–3 g/day as a universal muscle-building prescription; trial doses and products vary.
4. Ask a clinician or pharmacist before high-dose supplementation if you use anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication, have a bleeding disorder, are preparing for surgery, or have another relevant condition. The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) is a useful starting point.

## Bottom line

Omega-3s may have a modest, context-dependent role in strength or function, but the direct resistance-training evidence does not show a reliable increase in muscle mass. Treat fish oil as a nutrition or medical decision—not as a replacement for progressive training, protein, sleep, or calories.

## Limits of the evidence

The direct training literature is small, uses varied participants and products, and is more relevant to strength or function than proven hypertrophy. Supplement quality, background diet, medical status, and medication interactions also affect the decision.
